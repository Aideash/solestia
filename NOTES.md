Because we use apparent solar time rather than a uniform count, Mercury's hand genuinely creeps backwards for a few days around perihelion — its Sun reverses in the sky. I left that in as real behavior; switching to a mean Sun is a one-line change in localSolarTime if it reads as a glitch to you.

The clocks and P_rot share one longitude system, chosen at the top of the page (default IAU). IAU is WGCCRE 2015 / pck00011: System III for Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus, and Karkoschka's ~15.97 h south-polar cloud period for Neptune (the cartographic W since 2015). Magnetic is that same System III where IAU already is, and Voyager radio (~16.11 h, Seidelmann et al. 2002) for Neptune. Cloud is Jupiter System I, Saturn's equatorial System I (10h 14m; IAU no longer tabulates it), and Neptune's IAU 2015 W; Uranus and the inner planets have no separate cloud or magnetic W, so they stay on IAU. Jupiter System II is stored but not selectable.

JPL's satellite table calls its period column sidereal, but in a precessing-ellipse fit it is the period of the mean anomaly, and the apsidal and nodal periods beside it are unsigned. Take them at face value and Io comes out at 1.7627 d instead of the familiar 1.7691 d, and — worse — the mean longitude runs 1.48°/day fast, so the sub-Jupiter point walks right around the moon in about 240 days while still looking correct at J2000. The signs in `moons.ts` are therefore fixed by the one constraint the table cannot state: these moons are synchronous, so the sidereal mean motion has to equal the tabulated IAU spin rate. Exactly one sign pair does that per moon, each landing within 2e-4 deg/day, and it puts all four back on their textbook periods. Nodes regress throughout; the resonance-dominated inner pair have regressing apsides while Ganymede and Callisto advance. `check-kepler` asserts the lock still holds in 2049, which is the test the epoch-only check was missing.

Because the lock is exact by construction, the Jupiter day in the moon table is infinite rather than merely long: Jupiter hangs over the sub-Jupiter point and one hemisphere never sees it. The `lib` column carries the only motion left, the ±2e optical libration once per orbit. In the solar system the same 2e quantity is the eccentricity term of the equation of time, so it shares the column under the heading `eot` — ±7.66 min for Earth, ±11.6 d for Mercury. Both omit the obliquity term.

The Sun has a whisker in the orrery on the same footing as the planets, from the IAU pole and the Carrington W (sidereal 25.38 d). Treat that period as a label rather than a fact: the photosphere shears from roughly 24 d at the equator to 34 d near the poles, and Carrington picked his rate off sunspots near 16° latitude. So the Sun has no meaningful magnetic or cloud alternative and ignores the frame picker. If the table ever grows a shear column, this is the row that needs it most.
